# Teacher review: Island 3 (Who's trying to convince me?) and Island 4 (Pictures in words)

Scope: content/skills/3.1 to 3.11 and 4.1 to 4.10, every teach section and all 24 Encounters in each Skill. Choose options are shuffled at runtime, so answer position is not a cue. The content report is clean for every Skill. Sentence length rises by Band as planned, and Band 4 texts average about 190 to 210 words.

## 1. Biggest problems

### Problem 1. The I do names the move but never shows the thinking, and never names the trap the Encounters test

**Problem.** Every I do follows the same pattern: a short definition, then one highlighted phrase. No beat works through a hard case aloud ("Here's how I decide..."). The misconception that the Encounters test again and again is almost never named before practice. Students meet it for the first time in a Second look, after getting it wrong. Examples:
- 3.1: the number plus opinion word sentence ("Its ten tracks are the best").
- 3.4: the calm swap that changes the facts ("a few", "quiet").
- 3.6: not every omission is bias.
- 3.9: "must" meaning certain.
- 3.10: the answer has to fit the worry.
- 4.1: "as soon as" isn't a simile.
- 4.3: animals doing animal things aren't personification.
- 4.5: big true numbers aren't hyperbole.
- 4.6 to 4.10: "what effect" and "why this device".

Some beats are so compressed that an adult can't follow them. 3.4 beat 5 says "Closed is calm. Fixed is wrong!" and "Fixed" appears nowhere else. 3.1 beat 2 says "Look at the scoreboard" with nothing to look at. This is very likely what James felt when he played it.

**Why it matters.** A student who reads 18 months below level learns the rule from a single example and then generalises it too widely. A wrong first try, with no model to fall back on, also costs a "right first try" towards mastery.

**Evidence.** These are the explicit teaching moves in VTLM 2.0: modelling with worked examples, then checking for understanding before practice. The worked-example effect (Sweller and colleagues) is well established for novices. Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction also stress modelling, thinking aloud and checking understanding.

**Fix.** Give every I do the same three-part spine:
1. One worked example on the hard case, with the thinking spoken across two beats. For 3.1: "This one has a number AND an opinion word. Check each part." Then: "You can count ten tracks. You can't check 'the best'. One opinion part makes it an opinion."
2. One "Careful!" beat that names the trap with a minimal pair.
3. Make We do 1 test that exact trap.

### Problem 2. Most tasks can be solved from surface cues without using the Skill

**Problem.** Across both islands, a keyword or the shape of the options gives the answer away:
- Opinions always carry a superlative or "should" (3.1, 3.5).
- Experts always have "Dr" or a job title, evidence items say "records show", and examples start "Last Tuesday" (3.3).
- The emotive word is the only adjective among the taps (3.4).
- The rhetorical question is the only line with "?", and "everyone's doing it" always says "already" (3.7).
- Counter-arguments always read "Some say..., but" (3.10).
- Similes always have like or as, and metaphors always have "is" (4.1, 4.2).
- Lines name their own sense ("smelled", "tasted", "felt") (4.6).
- The wrong taps are "a plain fact, not a picture" (4.8).
- The right word is the only one about the person named in the prompt (4.9).
- The cliché is the shortest option (4.10).

The fix format has its own giveaway. Two taps are word-for-word quotes whose reasons say "That's right", so only one slot could be wrong (3.7, 4.5, 4.6, 4.8). In 3.7 the wrong label is always the duplicate.

**Why it matters.** Mastery is 4 of the last 5 right first try. A student can earn it by spotting keywords. They then miss the device when it has no signal word, or label a non-example as the device. The strongest readers are never stretched, and the weakest learn a shortcut instead of the concept.

**Evidence.** When an item can be answered from features that aren't the skill, it isn't measuring the skill. This is a basic point about assessment validity (Messick's "construct-irrelevant" variance). In concept teaching, examples and close non-examples presented together are what pin down a concept's edges. This is a core idea in Engelmann and Carnine's work on Direct Instruction.

**Fix.** From Band 2 up, every Encounter set should include two kinds of cue-breaker:
- a near non-example that carries the signal, for example a real question, "as soon as", a true "so... that", a "best-selling" fact, or a "Some say..., but whatever" with no real answer;
- a true example without the signal, for example an opinion with no "-est", a verb metaphor ("Mrs Patel buzzed"), a rebuttal with no "but", or imagery with no sense verb.

In fix items, every tap must need a judgement.

### Problem 3. Bands get longer, not harder, and the "why" half of each Skill is never tested

**Problem.** Bands 3 and 4 mostly add reading that has nothing to do with the answer: parking, opening hours, Gus jokes after the last tap, a 200-word passage when the options stand alone. The decision itself stays at Band 2 level.

Several Band 4 sorts are easier than the Band 2 choose items, because each item contains a literal word that names its pile ("wailed", "sweat dripped", "gleamed": 4.1-b4-5, 4.2-b4-4). Meanwhile the higher-order half of each Skill appears in the `why`, the `how` or the last beat, but almost no task asks it. Examples:
- Judge the backing (3.3): "Knowing the kind helps you judge it".
- Is the claim true? (3.7)
- Why this line for this reader? (3.8)
- How strong is the rebuttal? (3.10)
- Compare the effects of two images (4.1, 4.2).
- What picture does it make? (4.6)

The few real stretches show what Band 4 could be everywhere: 3.4-b4-1, 3.8-b4-1, 3.9-b4-6, 3.11-b4-1, 3.11-b4-6, 4.8-b4-5, 4.9-b4-3 and 4.10-b4-5.

**Why it matters.** A weak reader pays for irrelevant text in stamina and gives up. A strong reader masters the Skill without meeting anything at Level 6. The codes these Skills claim use the verbs explain, compare and examine (VC2E5LE04 "compare the effects", VC2E5LY08 "explain characteristic features", VC2E6LE04 "explain the way authors use sound and imagery"). Labelling alone doesn't meet them.

**Evidence.** Cognitive load theory: text that doesn't bear on the task is extraneous load, and it hurts novices most. Also curriculum alignment with the stated achievement standard.

**Fix.** Write one Band ladder per Skill and hold every Encounter to it:
- Band 1: spot it, with the signal present.
- Band 2: spot it without the signal, or say what it means.
- Band 3: spot it among cue-breaking decoys, and say what effect it has.
- Band 4: judge or compare. Which is stronger, which fits this reader, which works better, and why.

Cap context at the sentences the task needs plus two lines of story.

**Also island-wide (terminology).** Several labels clash with how Australian classrooms name things:
- "Fact" is used for false claims (3.1, 3.5).
- "Evidence" means numbers only (3.3).
- Modality is called strong/medium/weak, using three different labelling schemes (3.9).
- Counter-argument and rebuttal are never named (3.10).
- Exaggeration (3.7) and hyperbole (4.5) are taught as if unrelated.
- "Literally" and "stretch the truth" carry most Island 4 feedback but are never defined, and "stretch the truth" is itself an idiom.

Each is in the tables below.

## 3. What's good

- The world is the hook. Maxx as a slightly sinister influencer, Mo's fish-finger custard, Pebble the rover and the running campaigns across Skills make the reading feel like a story, not a worksheet. The Australian settings and idioms are a real strength. Many items also teach real self-protection, such as Maxx's data-harvesting email (3.8-b4-5) and the small-print "may" (3.9-b3-6).
- The best items show what every Band 4 could be: 3.4-b4-1 (emotive, falsely softened and neutral versions side by side), 3.5-b4-6 (subjective despite a number), 3.9-b4-6 (might to must as the evidence builds), 3.11-b4-1 and 3.11-b4-6 (criteria that conflict), 4.5-b3-4 (big numbers can be true), 4.9-b4-3 (slender or scrawny) and 4.10-b4-5 (a cliché used ironically).
- Most Second look reasons quote the text and say why an option fails without naming the answer (3.10-b3-4). 4.7's "Say 'drum' and 'bass' aloud. They don't rhyme" gives an action, not the answer. That is the model for 4.6 and the 3.3 sorts.
