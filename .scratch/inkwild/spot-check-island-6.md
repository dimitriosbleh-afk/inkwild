# Spot check: Comma Cay (Punctuation for meaning)

These are the 10 Encounters the automatic checks were least sure about. For each, tick **OK**, or write what is wrong. Two right answers, a confusing prompt, or a wrong answer are all worth a note.

## 1. 6.3-b3-5 (Band 3)



**Match each sentence to the right rock.**

- Inside Mo's lunchbox, a sandwich had grown fur. → **punctuated right**
- Inside Mo's, lunchbox a sandwich had grown fur. → **comma inside the opening phrase**
- Inside Mo's lunchbox a sandwich, had grown fur. → **comma after the doer**
- Behind the school bins, Indi found Mo's left sock. → **punctuated right**
- Under the couch cushions, Zed found six chips. → **punctuated right**
- Under Mo's bed sheets and socks lay in a heap. → **no comma at all**
- Under the couch cushions Zed, found six chips. → **comma after the doer**

Why: The comma goes after the whole opening phrase: not inside it, and not after the doer.

## 2. 6.3-b4-3 (Band 4)



**What does the comma after 'lab' do for the reader?**

Options: It ends the 'when' part, so readers know when the robots danced. / It stops readers from thinking the lab itself was dancing. / It ends a sentence that makes sense alone, so a capital could follow. / **It ends the 'where' part, so readers don't see 'lab robots'.** (answer)

Why: The comma marks where the opener ends. Without it, you might read 'lab robots' and lose the place.

**Make the sentence say the Stashlet ate four things. Start the list with honey and end with berries.**

Pieces: The Stashlet ate | honey | cake | nuts | and | berries | , | , | , | .

Answer: **The Stashlet ate honey, cake, nuts and berries.** or **The Stashlet ate honey, nuts, cake and berries.**

Why: The comma after 'honey' splits honey from cake, so the list shows four things, not three.
