# Teacher review: island 6 (Punctuation for meaning) and island 7 (How texts hang together)

Reviewer group: island-6-7. Files read: content/skills/6.1 to 6.10 and 7.1 to 7.12, every beat, We do task and Encounter, plus docs/curriculum/skill-list.md and the VC v2.0 code text. I read each Skill as three students: one 18 months below level, one on level, one 18 months ahead.

## 1. Biggest problems

### Problem 1: The I do states a rule but never shows the thinking, and the Encounters then test things the I do never taught

**The problem.** Almost every I do is four or five one-line rules with a bolded example. None of them walks through a decision ("I look for the doer... it starts at 'the ferry'... so the comma goes just before it"). The mistake students actually make is rarely named in the I do:
- comma splices (6.1: the main distractor in the Encounters, but absent from the I do)
- the difference between a phrase and a clause (6.3 and 6.4 look identical to a child)
- defining clauses that must not take commas (6.5)
- an apostrophe on any plural (6.7)
- "it's" for owning, because 's means owning (6.9)
- "don't repeat words" (7.10)
- "it" points to the nearest noun (7.9)

Then Encounters test content the I do never taught:
- the lowercase second half of split speech (6.10)
- present tense for reports (7.3)
- Nevertheless, Consequently and Furthermore (7.8)
- stage names (7.2)
- formality as a scale, not a switch (7.12)

The only check before practice is the two We do tasks, and in 6.7 to 6.10 each one has just two options, so it is a coin toss.

**Why it matters.** This is why James found it hard to follow as an adult. A rule with no modelled decision leaves the student to work out the method from their own wrong answers. The strong reader will manage that. The student below level will not, and will learn from the Second look that they are bad at punctuation.

**Evidence.** The worked-example effect from cognitive load theory (Sweller and colleagues): novices learn more from studying a fully worked example than from solving problems with only a rule. Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction: present new material in small steps, provide models, check for understanding before independent practice. Both sit behind the Explicit Teaching element of VTLM 2.0.

**The one move.** Give every I do the same three-part shape:
1. A think-aloud beat that works one example step by step in the crew member's voice.
2. A trap beat that shows the common wrong version and says why it's wrong.
3. A We do 1 with three options, one of which is that trap.

Then check each Encounter against the beats. If it needs an idea the beats don't teach, add a beat or move the Encounter.

### Problem 2: Students can score without using the Skill, and the Second look often hands them the answer

**The problem.**
- **Only one plausible tap.** In tapText and fix, only the bracketed parts can be tapped. In the Band 1 and 2 tasks of 6.1 to 6.4, the decoy taps are usually "the start, it already has a capital" and "the end, the full stop is already there", which leaves one real target. In 6.5 the answer is always the chunk between the two commas that are already printed. In the 6.9 fix tasks only one word is an its/it's or who's/whose. In 7.5 the answer is always the one opener that breaks a pattern the text has just announced.
- **Distractors nobody writes.** Many wrong options put commas where no child would ("At, dawn", "On the, ice the penguins slid"). Students learn to spot a silly comma, not to place a comma.
- **Second looks that give the answer.** About 130 of the roughly 400 wrong-answer reasons in 6.1 to 6.5 end by naming the exact word ("It goes after 'mountain'."). The second try becomes copying.

**Why it matters.** Mastery counts right first tries. If the shape of the task gives the answer, the game reports learning that hasn't happened, and the student moves on without the move. The Second look is the best teaching moment in the game, and naming the location throws it away.

**Evidence.** It is a standard assessment-design principle that distractors should come from real student errors (often called diagnostic distractors), so that a wrong pick tells you what the student believes. On feedback, Hattie and Timperley's model ("The power of feedback", 2007) shows that feedback about the process (how to find the answer) does more for learning than feedback that just supplies the answer.

**The one move.** Adopt one writing rule for every task:
- Each tap or choice has at least two wrong answers a real 10 to 12 year old might pick.
- Each Second look reason names the principle, then sends the student back with a question ("Where does the whole 'where' part finish?"). It never names the location.

### Problem 3: In island 7, the Bands get longer, not harder

**The problem.** Average passage length climbs from about 65 words at Band 1 to about 205 at Band 4 in 7.1, 7.2, 7.3, 7.4, 7.9, 7.10 and 7.11 (from content-report.ts). Many Band 3 and 4 texts open with a preamble that repeats the frame inside the passage. For example, 7.5-b4-1 has about 250 words of set-up before one choice of opening line. Meanwhile the move itself is often the same at every Band:
- 7.5: spot the opener that breaks an announced pattern
- 7.7: find the sentence that starts with a time or place phrase
- 7.11: each text announces "X usually looks like this, this one doesn't"
- order tasks: grow to six or seven sentences of 20+ words

**Why it matters.** For the student 18 months below level and many EAL learners, Band 3 becomes a reading-stamina test and they never reach the Skill. For the student 18 months ahead, Band 4 is the Band 1 task with more words, which is no stretch and no fun. A child choosing between this and a novel will not wade through 250 words of preamble for one tap.

**Evidence.** Cognitive load theory again. Load that comes from the passage rather than the thing being learnt (extraneous load) uses up working memory without building the skill. Difficulty should come from the skill itself (element interactivity), not from length.

**The one move.**
- Cap Band 3 passages at about 100 words and Band 4 at about 150, and move all set-up into `frame`.
- Write each Band's step-up into the Skill: remove the signpost (7.7), remove the "usually..." scaffold (7.11), add a near-miss distractor (7.4, 7.12), or ask why rather than what (7.11).

## 3. What's good

- The humour and recurring cast are genuinely fun and worth keeping: Grandma escaping dinner, Mo's cooking, Maxx's fake "fact sheet" (7.1-b4-1), Mo's love letter to a pie (7.11-b4-3) and the logbook ghost story (7.11-b4-1). The jokes usually carry the teaching point, which is exactly right.
- The best tasks test meaning, which is what punctuation is for. Do more of these: 6.1-b4-6 (did the coach sleep through the match?), 6.2-b2-5 (count the teammates), 6.4-b2-5 and 6.4-b4-5 (main clause first, no comma), 6.6-b4-1 ("time travellers"). 6.8's "write the owners first, then ask: does it end in s?" and 7.6's "find the sentence that needs nothing before it" are expert moves, modelled in the right order.
- Most wrong-option reasons in island 7 are specific, kind and about the student's thinking (7.4's "that's one detail, not the main idea", 7.9's swap test). That is the standard to bring island 6's Second looks up to.
