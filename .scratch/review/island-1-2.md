# Teacher review: Islands 1 and 2 (Reading between the lines, Whose story is it?)

Files reviewed: content/skills/1.1 to 1.9 and 2.1 to 2.10 (19 Skills, 456 Encounters, 38 We do tasks), against docs/curriculum/skill-list.md and vc2-english-levels-4-7.md. Content report run for prefixes 1. and 2.

## 1. Biggest problems

### Problem 1: The I do states a rule. It doesn't teach the thinking, and nothing checks it landed.

**The problem.** Every I do is 4 or 5 one-line beats around a single tiny example. The thinking is half-narrated: the crew member asks the key question and answers it in the next beat (1.2: "When do people slam doors?" then "When they're angry!"). None of the 19 Skills has a check for understanding before We do. The common misconception is named well in only a few (2.1 "an I inside speech marks doesn't count", 1.9 "if both texts say it, it isn't new", 1.7 narrow/broad/untrue, 2.10 "true, but does that show she's brave?"). Elsewhere it is missing. Sometimes the Encounters then test an idea the I do never taught:
- 1.9 asks "How does Text B connect to Text A?" from Band 2 (an example, a different explanation, an update, a correction). Only "what's new" was taught.
- 1.6 Band 4 marks "the topic" wrong. The I do defined main idea as the topic.
- 2.5 Band 4 sorts problems "inside" and "outside" the character. That was never introduced.
- 1.4 is "predict, then check", but the student never checks.

James found the teaching hard to follow as an adult. This is why: there is a rule, one example and then practice, with nothing in between.

**Why it matters.** The student reading 18 months below level needs to see the whole move done slowly, once, with the thinking out loud. Then they need to try one tiny version and be told whether they've got it. Without that, the We do turns into trial and error, and the "Second look" does the teaching after the student has already got it wrong. The student who is ahead loses nothing from a 20-second check.

**The evidence.** The worked-example effect (Sweller and colleagues): novices learn more from studying fully worked examples than from solving problems straight away. Checking for understanding during teaching is one of Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction, and it sits inside Explicit Teaching in VTLM 2.0. Pairing an example with a non-example is a standard concept-teaching move. It is central to Engelmann's Direct Instruction.

**The one move.** Give every I do the same three-part spine:
1. A worked example with the thinking said aloud.
2. A non-example beat that names the misconception.
3. One quick-check tap or choose on a new tiny example, with a misconception distractor and feedback, before We do 1.

Where Encounters test a sub-idea (1.9 relationships, 1.6 topic vs main idea, 2.5 inside/outside problems), add one beat that teaches it.

### Problem 2: The answer can usually be found from the shape of the options, and Bands step up in length, not thinking.

**The problem.** Across both islands, many tasks can be passed without doing the Skill:
- **Far-off distractors.** "bored" is a distractor 7 times in 1.2. Far-off "lazy/shy" traits recur in 2.4, and "lunchtime" is offered when the moon is out in 2.6. 1.5 meanings are usually opposites ("whispered quietly" for bellowed).
- **Templates.** Every "too broad" summary in 1.7 is the same shape ("This story is about an animal", "A musician has been in the local news"). "Neither text gives a price/cost/tickets" is a distractor in 6 tasks in 1.9.
- **Tells.**
  - Every "can't know" sentence in 2.2 opens with a jump to somewhere else: "Inside her head", "Out in the dark", "Down in the hall", "Over in Perth", "Right now, in every lounge room". The knowable ones say "I could hear" or "told me".
  - In fix tasks the wrong word is nearly always the skill word in the last slot: "He felt [X]" (all 4 in 1.2), "because [X]" (1.3), "In Tully's telling he seems [X]" (2.3).
  - The order tasks in 2.10 can be solved from the connectives alone ("For example", "That shows", "However").
  - The correct choose answer is the longest option in about half the tasks in 1.2, 2.9 and 2.10, and in all 4 fix tasks in 1.7.
- **Sort feedback gives the answer.** The Second look for a misplaced sort item names the pile: "Sunday is a day, so it tells you when", "There are no speech marks, so this I is the teller".
- **Length does the stepping up.** The content report shows almost the same profile in every Skill: about 65, 105, 160 and 210 words for Bands 1 to 4. In 1.5, 1.8, 2.2, 2.6 and 2.9 the Band 4 move is the Band 1 move on a longer text. 1.5 Band 4 even defines the word in the text ("a cacophony... It was a horrible jumble of clashing sounds").

Some Skills do step up in thinking, and they are the best ones: 1.2, 1.3, 1.6, 1.9, 2.4 and 2.10.

**Why it matters.** Mastery is 4 of the last 5 right first try. If the shape gives the answer, students are marked as having mastered a Skill they don't have, and the adaptive Band can't find their real level. Length-only stepping punishes the weak decoder, who may infer well. It also never stretches the strong reader, who reads 210 words easily but never meets a near-miss.

**The evidence.** Messick called this construct-irrelevant variance: a score driven by something other than the skill being measured. The well-known multiple-choice item-writing guidelines (Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez reviewed them) say all distractors should be plausible, should come from common errors, and shouldn't be cued by length or wording. I'm confident of the idea. Check the exact paper title before quoting it.

**The one move.** Make distractor closeness the main difficulty dial, and keep length as a minor one:
- Band 1: one plausible distractor and one far-off.
- Band 2: two plausible distractors.
- Band 3: a distractor that fits some clues but not all.
- Band 4: a near-miss that is true in the text but answers a different question, or names the commonest misconception.

At the same time, rewrite sort reasons as prompts ("Is this I inside or outside speech marks?"), not answers.

### Problem 3: Some of the metalanguage clashes with what Victorian classrooms teach, or is simply wrong.

**The problem.**
- **2.8.** "Complication" means "what makes the problem worse", and the problem comes first. In most Australian classrooms the narrative structure is orientation, complication, resolution, and the complication *is* the problem. I believe the NAPLAN narrative marking guide uses those terms too; please check. A student who answers as their teacher taught is marked wrong ("That's the problem at the start").
- **1.6.**
  - The I do defines main idea as "what the whole text is about", which is the topic. Band 4 then marks the topic wrong.
  - Five sort tasks file supporting details in a pile called "interesting detail". One reason even says "It supports the main idea" while filing it there.
- **2.5.** "The want plus the problem. That's the dilemma." A dilemma is a hard choice between two things. Most tasks are plain obstacles (spinach pies, a fence).
- **2.7.** Four texts are dated in the future (2090 to 2350), and the student is told they show "life in 2150". A text can only reflect the time it was written in.
- **2.2.** "A teller can't know everything." That is untrue of third-person narrators, and it contradicts 2.1-b3-1.
- **Island 2.** It says "teller" throughout. VC2 and teachers say "narrator", and students never meet the word.
- **2.10.** The Skill is "with a feature of the text", but most evidence is plot events, not features.

**Why it matters.** Students carry these words between the iPad and the classroom. Two names for one thing, or one name for two things, causes confusion and erodes trust in the game when the teacher says "that's not what complication means". VC2E5LE02 explicitly asks students to use metalanguage, so the words have to be the right ones. This is less a research finding than plain consistency, so I won't dress it up as one.

**The one move.** Do one terminology pass against Victorian classroom usage, and record the agreed terms in GLOSSARY.md:
- narrator (teller)
- orientation, complication, resolution
- topic vs main idea
- supporting detail vs fun extra
- dilemma means a choice
- language feature

Then fix the affected Skills in the same pass. The table rows below give replacement lines.

### 2.8 Plot shape and theme

| where | severity | kind | problem | fix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| teach beats 2-3, weDo 1, b1-1, b2-1, b3-6, b4-2 | blocker | accuracy | The game says the problem comes first and the complication is "what makes it worse". ## 3. What's good

- The writing is genuinely funny and very Australian (Mo's cooking, Maxx Vantablack's fake rescues, payoff success lines like 1.4's "Into the wheelie bin it went"). The running Maxx storyline gives the inference and point-of-view Skills a real-world point: spotting a liar. That is the strongest reason a 10 to 12 year old would pick this over a book.
- Where Band 4 steps up in thinking, it is excellent and worth copying across both islands:
  - 1.2 hidden and mixed feelings
  - 1.3 multi-clue causes
  - 1.6 implied main ideas
  - 1.9 the press release against the lifeguards, and the gallery label against the artist's letter
  - 2.1 an object narrator and "that hand was mine"
  - 2.4 "gruff but caring"
  - 2.10 word choice making a villain
- Most choose-task Second look reasons are specific and send the student back to a named clue in the text. Keep that voice, and bring the sort reasons up to it.
