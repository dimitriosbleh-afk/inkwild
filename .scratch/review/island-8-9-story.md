# Review: island 8 (Editing desk), island 9 (Story workshop) and the story Chapters

Reviewed: content/skills/8.1 to 8.9, content/skills/9.1 to 9.10, all 23 files in content/chapters, docs/story/bible.md, docs/curriculum/skill-list.md (codes checked against vc2-english-levels-4-7.md). I read every island 8 Encounter and all of island 9. For the Chapters I read Bands 1 and 4 of every Chapter in full, sampled Band 2, and counted words and sentences with a rough script. Word and sentence figures below are my counts, not the game's.

## 1. Biggest problems

### 1. The island 8 I do states rules, and four of those rules are wrong or will need unteaching

**Problem.** Each I do is four or five one-line rules with an example. None shows the thinking an editor does on a real draft, and the common trap is almost never named. Worse, several rules are the misconception itself, and the Band 3 and 4 Encounters then mark students wrong for following them:
- 8.3: "A story picks a time and sticks to it." But b3-3, b4-1 and b4-3 need the student to know that facts and "Tonight, I'm writing this" are rightly in the present.
- 8.2: "A sentence is one whole thought." But the right answers join two whole thoughts with ", and". The comma splice is the run-on Year 5 and 6 writers make most, and it is the wrong option in most 8.2 items, yet the I do never shows it.
- 8.1: "The doer should come first." Passive sentences ("The goal was kicked by Tully") are correct English.
- 8.4: "Repeats make readers drift off", with no exception, while island 7 (7.10) teaches repetition that holds a text together.
- 8.7 teaches end marks and capitals, then Bands 2 to 4 test its/it's, commas around extra information and split speech, none of which it mentions.

This is why James found the teaching hard to follow as an adult. The rules are short, but they don't match what the tasks ask.

**Why it matters.** The student 18 months below level holds onto the rule exactly as stated. When Band 3 says the rule is wrong, the student doesn't learn a better rule. They decide the game is unfair. The strong student notices the contradiction and stops trusting the teaching.

**Evidence idea.** The worked-example effect (Sweller and colleagues): novices learn more from studying a fully worked example with the reasoning shown than from a rule followed by practice. Also the plain classroom principle that a misconception should be named and dealt with during teaching, not discovered through wrong answers.

**The one move.** Give every island 8 I do one worked beat that thinks aloud through a near miss and names the trap, and rewrite the four wrong rules. For example, 8.3: "Careful. Some lines are meant to be now. 'Lava is hot' is always true, so it stays present. Only fix the verb that breaks the draft's time." The exact lines are in the Skill notes.

### 2. Too many island 8 tasks can be won from the shape of the options

**Problem.** Across the island, the right answer can often be found without using the Skill:
- 8.9: the wrong options are usually false statements about the edit ("Edit A never names a goal kicker", "Edit B never mentions the meteor shower"). A student can check the facts and never think about the reader.
- 8.5: distractors are mostly opposites (steaming/icy, soft/deafening, brand-new/worn-out).
- 8.6: the prompt names the topic ("...in Wren's paragraph about koalas sleeping"), so the student skips the first step the I do teaches. The stray sentence is nearly always a crew member's opinion or family ("Zed's mum says...", "Indi's mum reckons...").
- 8.3: the slipped verb is always the only verb in a different tense, from Band 1 to Band 4.
- Twenty-four sort prompts start "Match each...". Twelve of them end "to the right bin" or "to the right pile", the generic prompt the brief warns about.

**Why it matters.** A Skill is mastered at 4 of the last 5 right first try. If the items can be passed by spotting the odd one out, "mastered" means "good at spotting the odd one out". Then the student meets a real draft, their own, where nothing stands out.

**Evidence idea.** Retrieval practice and "desirable difficulties" (Bjork): practice builds the thinking it actually requires. If surface cues are enough, students practise the cues. This is a general principle, not a claim about a specific study of this game.

**The one move.** At Bands 3 and 4, make every wrong option a true, plausible near miss that is wrong for a reason tied to the Skill. Examples: in 8.9, "Edit B, because it shows how proud Tully felt" (true, but fans who missed the game need the result). In 8.6, use same-subject strays like the wombat lifespan in the I do. Remove the topic from 8.6 prompts from Band 2 up.

### 3. Island 9 goes from spotting to a blank page and ends with a tick-box, and no one ever edits their own writing

**Problem.** In every workshop:
- Both We do tasks are recognition (choose or sort). The student never builds, fixes or finishes a line of the target writing.
- The student then faces a blank page with a list of ideas. There is no starter line, no size of task ("three sentences") and no challenge for a strong writer.
- The checklist is self-ticked, and several items can't be checked ("I made my reader feel like they're there", "I picked surprising details").

Meanwhile island 8 practises editing only on other people's drafts, by design. VC2E5LY11 and VC2E6LY10 both say "re-read and edit their own texts and the texts of others". The "own texts" half isn't anywhere in the game, and island 9 is the only place it could be. 8.8 teaches the right move for a checklist ("for each item, find the proof in the draft"), but island 9's checklists never ask for it.

**Why it matters.** A reluctant 11 year old stalls at the blank page, not at the mentor text. A tick-box with vague items gets ticked whatever was written, so it gives no feedback. With no teacher and no AI, the student's own re-read is the only feedback loop this design has. At the moment it isn't used.

**Evidence idea.** Graham and Perin's *Writing Next* (2007) meta-analysis names explicit strategy instruction, specific product goals, studying models and sentence combining among the most effective approaches for this age. Self-Regulated Strategy Development (Harris and Graham) is well supported for struggling writers and builds in modelling, goal setting and self-checking. The general point that practice on others' drafts doesn't transfer to one's own writing by itself is well known, but I'm not citing a specific study for it.

**The one move.** Make every workshop the same three steps:
1. **Borrow.** Make We do 2 a fix or build task that produces one line of the target writing.
2. **Write.** Give a small, specific goal with a starter line, plus an optional challenge.
3. **Read it back.** Replace two checklist items with island 8 checks on the student's own piece. For example: "Find one 'and then'. Cut it or change it." "Find one blurry word (good, nice, thing, stuff). Swap it." "Tap the line that proves you used a sound."

## Chapters (story reward)

**Is it good reading?** Yes. Band 4 is genuinely funny, with real comic timing ("Nobody cheers, exactly, but Mo quietly pushes the custard away, which is almost the same thing."). The literacy jokes are built into the plot ("Let's eat, Madame Pause", the cut NOT, "Stop them bots"), so they feel earned rather than bolted on. The ending (MAX. One X. "One chance." "One's enough.") lands.

**Would a 10 to 12 year old want the next Chapter?** At first, yes. The end hooks are strong ("See you soon, Captain", "You're live", something crying behind the door). But every island pair runs the same skeleton, so by the fourth Heist the page is predictable and the Old Glimmer hook has already been used up.

**Is Band 1 readable by a weak reader?** Mostly yes. Sentences average about 5 words, it's in the present tense, and the vocabulary is mostly plain. The risks are the number of names and three places where Band 1 drops a piece of the story the ending depends on (Sir Nibbles, "You promised", the comma joke).

**Does Band 4 stretch a strong reader?** Not much. It has richer words and fuller scenes, but the same events and the same explicitly stated feelings, with sentences averaging about 7 to 11 words by my count. Across the whole year there is also very little of it.

**Anything inappropriate or confusing?** Nothing inappropriate. The gross humour, mild peril and the betrayal are well judged for 10 to 12 year olds, and Maxx's apology and redemption are handled well. One thing a careful parent might ask is where the adults are while a runaway child joins a crew of children. A single line (a Keeper or a gran who knows where Wren is) would close it. ## 3. What's good

- Island 8's Second look reasons are specific and mostly don't give the answer away. The distractors are real student errors (comma splices, "its'", "five dollar's", "Although... but"). 8.8's "find the proof for each item" is the clearest strategy in the island.
- Island 9's mentor texts are short, funny and Australian (Nan pointing a sausage roll at the umpire, the troll who lost his daisies, the growling bin), and the prompts mix the crew's world with kids' own.
- The Chapters' Band 4 prose is a pleasure to read. The literacy jokes driving the plot ("Let's eat, Madame Pause", the cut NOT) are the best idea in the whole story. Keep them while you fix the formula.
